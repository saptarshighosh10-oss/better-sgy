How a cell turns food into usable energy

By the Biology Notes team · 9 min read · Updated June 20, 2026

Every cell runs on a tiny chemical currency called ATP. Cellular respiration is the process that keeps that currency in circulation — breaking glucose down, step by step, to release the energy stored in its bonds.

It is easy to picture respiration as a single event, but it unfolds across three connected stages. Each one hands its products to the next, and each is tuned to capture as much energy as possible before the leftovers are released.

Glycolysis: the first split

Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm and needs no oxygen. A single glucose molecule is split into two molecules of pyruvate, and the cell comes away with a small but immediate net gain of ATP. It is the oldest part of the pathway, shared by nearly every living thing.

The whole point is patience: respiration releases glucose's energy in small, controlled steps rather than one wasteful burst.

The Krebs cycle and electron transport

Inside the mitochondria, pyruvate is processed through the Krebs cycle, and the high-energy electrons it produces are passed along the electron transport chain. This last stage is where the bulk of the cell's ATP is finally assembled, with oxygen as the final electron acceptor.

For Friday's quiz, focus on which stage produces the most ATP and on the role oxygen plays at the very end. Those two ideas tie the whole pathway together — and they are the ones that show up most often on the test.
