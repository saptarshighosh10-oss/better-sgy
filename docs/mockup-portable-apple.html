Study Guide · Unit 5

How cells turn food into usable energy

By the Biology Notes team · 9 min read · Updated June 20

Every cell runs on a tiny chemical currency called ATP. Cellular respiration is the process that keeps that currency in circulation — breaking down glucose, step by step, to release the energy stored in its bonds.

It is easy to picture respiration as a single event, but it unfolds across three connected stages. Each one hands its products to the next, and each one is tuned to capture as much energy as possible before the leftovers are released. Understanding where each stage happens inside the cell is half the work of understanding why it happens at all.

Glycolysis: the first split

Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen. A single molecule of glucose is split into two molecules of pyruvate, and the cell comes away with a small but immediate net gain of ATP. This is the oldest part of the pathway, shared by nearly every living thing, which is why it is a useful place to start.

Figure 1 — A simplified mitochondrion, where the later stages take place.

The Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain

Inside the mitochondria, pyruvate is processed through the Krebs cycle, and the high-energy electrons it produces are passed along the electron transport chain. This last stage is where the bulk of the cell's ATP is finally assembled, using oxygen as the final electron acceptor.

The whole point is patience: respiration releases glucose's energy in small, controlled steps rather than one wasteful burst.

For the quiz on Friday, focus on which stage produces the most ATP and on the role oxygen plays at the very end. Those two ideas tie the whole pathway together — and they are the ones that show up most often on the test.
