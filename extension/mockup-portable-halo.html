Spacing it out: why studying a little at a time beats cramming

By Dana Okafor · 9 min read · Updated June 20, 2026

Most students discover the night before an exam that there is more material than hours left in the evening. The temptation is to push through in one long sitting. Decades of research on memory suggest this is close to the least efficient way to learn anything you actually want to keep.

The effect has a name: spaced repetition. When you revisit material across several shorter sessions instead of one marathon, more of it survives the week, the month, and the final. The gaps between sessions are not wasted time. They are part of how the memory consolidates.

"The forgetting is the point. Each time you almost lose a fact and then recover it, the recovery is what makes it stick."

Why the gap helps

During a single long session, the second hour rarely teaches you much the first hour did not. Attention drifts, and rereading starts to feel like progress without being progress. A short break — even a day — lets the trace fade just enough that retrieving it again counts as real practice.

Researchers call this desirable difficulty. Easy review feels good and does little. Slightly effortful review feels worse and does more. The discomfort of nearly forgetting is a feature, not a bug.

Putting it to use

Practically, this means breaking a topic into a handful of fifteen-minute reviews spread across a week, rather than a single two-hour block the night before. Self-testing beats rereading. Mixing related topics, rather than drilling one at a time, helps too.

None of this requires special tools. A calendar, a stack of questions, and the discipline to start a few days early will do. The hardest part is trusting that the slower path is the faster one.
